# College Legends

> Build the program. Develop the talent. Grow the empire. Stay solvent.

**College Legends** is an original college-football business simulation currently in active early development.

It is not a conventional dynasty mode where winning games automatically solves everything. The player takes control of a financially vulnerable fictional football program and must build both a successful team and a sustainable institution.

Recruit overlooked players. Develop talent. Assemble compatible coaching staffs. Prepare competitive seasons. Sell tickets and merchandise. Negotiate media deals. Build departments, stores, facilities, and events. Expand carefully—because winning irresponsibly can still bankrupt the program.

## Development status

College Legends is in **active early development**. The core vision and major gameplay systems are established, while prototypes and implementation are underway.

This README describes the intended game. Features listed below should be treated as planned unless specifically identified as implemented in future development updates.

## The core fantasy

You begin with a struggling program:

- Limited cash and weak facilities
- Inexperienced staff
- Poor regional recognition
- Incomplete scouting information
- Little merchandise demand
- Few media opportunities
- No margin for repeated mistakes

Over time, the program can grow into a football empire with:

- Elite players and coaches
- Advanced scouting, analytics, medical, and development departments
- A major stadium and training complex
- Regional merchandise stores and distribution
- Owned radio, digital, and broadcast properties
- Camps, combines, showcase games, and recurring events
- National sponsorship, licensing, and media agreements

The institution—not merely the team—is the product.

## Core gameplay loop

Each in-game week follows a connected management loop:

1. Inspect new information and forecasts.
2. Choose where to invest limited money, staff, and time.
3. Advance the calendar as projects progress simultaneously.
4. Observe results shaped by preparation, compatibility, and uncertainty.
5. Generate revenue—or absorb losses.
6. Reinvest, restructure, or change strategy.

At any moment, the player may be recruiting prospects, developing position groups, preparing a season, marketing star players, selling tickets, producing merchandise, building facilities, negotiating media contracts, and defending a lawsuit.

That controlled overload is the heart of College Legends.

## Major systems

### Recruiting and scouting

Discover high-school players, junior-college prospects, transfers, international athletes, coaches, and support staff.

Scouting reveals information gradually. A prospect may appear talented but carry hidden risks involving work ethic, durability, academics, personality, scheme fit, financial expectations, or transfer likelihood. Better scouts improve accuracy; they do not eliminate uncertainty.

### Player development

Talent must be developed, not merely collected.

Choose individual or position-group development plans involving technical skill, strength, speed, tactical understanding, leadership, rehabilitation, academics, and media training. Results depend on the player, coach, facilities, intensity, compatibility, fatigue, and chance.

### Coaches, schemes, and fit

The right combination matters more than any single rating.

A player's outcome is influenced by:

- Natural talent and potential
- Head coach and coordinators
- Position coaches
- Tactical system
- Teammates and chemistry
- Development plan
- Available resources
- Uncertainty

A gifted quarterback can fail in the wrong system. An overlooked player can become a legend under the right staff.

### Team preparation and seasons

A season is the program's flagship product.

The player evaluates the roster, hires staff, installs schemes, conducts training camp, sets the depth chart, builds the schedule, prices tickets, launches marketing, and prepares merchandise before competition begins.

Each season creates lasting results:

- Wins, losses, rankings, and postseason outcomes
- Attendance and broadcast audience
- Recruiting and recognition effects
- Merchandise and ticket revenue
- Awards and historical significance
- Profit or loss

### Financial management

Money should remain difficult even after the team becomes successful.

Revenue may come from tickets, media rights, merchandise, sponsorships, donors, events, camps, stores, licensing, and conference distributions.

Expenses include payroll, recruiting, development, travel, facilities, inventory, marketing, insurance, legal costs, debt service, retail operations, media operations, and taxes.

Major investments also create ongoing obligations. A new facility can improve recruiting and development while its loan payments, maintenance, staffing, and insurance drain cash for years.

Sustained insolvency can end the game. The current design uses an eight-year financial failure window, allowing deliberate investment periods without making reckless expansion consequence-free.

### Merchandising and retail

Create program, player, rivalry, retro, mascot, and championship collections.

Choose suppliers, manufacturing quality, inventory, pricing, distribution, marketing, and athlete revenue share. Ordering too little sacrifices sales. Ordering too much traps cash in inventory—especially if a star transfers or the team loses the game that merchandise was built around.

Successful programs can expand from stadium kiosks to campus stores, regional flagships, fulfillment centers, and national licensing.

### Scheduling, tickets, and events

Build schedules as athletic and financial projects.

Negotiate home-and-home series, neutral-site showcases, rivalry events, international games, camps, combines, and recurring football festivals. Each opportunity carries revenue potential, travel costs, fatigue, injury risk, exposure, opponent difficulty, and uncertain demand.

Eventually, the program can create and operate its own branded events.

### Media and recognition

Negotiate with radio stations, broadcasters, streaming services, and publications. A larger program can acquire media assets of its own.

Recognition affects recruiting, attendance, sponsorships, merchandise, awards, and negotiating leverage—but owned media also brings payroll, equipment, taxes, regulation, and legal exposure.

### Departments and delegation

The player begins by handling most decisions directly, then builds an organization.

Planned departments include:

- Recruiting and scouting
- Player development
- Sports medicine
- Analytics
- Marketing and merchandising
- Media and broadcasting
- Finance and fundraising
- Legal and compliance
- Academic support and human resources
- Retail, facilities, and event operations

Executives differ in skill, capacity, efficiency, forecast accuracy, loyalty, personality, and ambition. Strong leaders make scale possible, but elite executives may be unaffordable for smaller programs.

### Legal, compliance, and taxes

Operational decisions can create lawsuits and regulatory problems involving injuries, contracts, employment, facilities, sponsors, merchandise, broadcasts, or athlete likenesses.

Cases develop over time. The player can settle, fight, mediate, use insurance, or restructure the responsible operation. Better legal and finance teams improve estimates and options without guaranteeing outcomes.

### History and legacy

Every season becomes part of a persistent program archive.

The game will track records, rankings, revenue, profit, attendance, audiences, recruiting classes, awards, coaching staffs, notable players, major upsets, and merchandise successes.

The goal is not only to win the next game. It is to create players, teams, and eras that remain meaningful decades later.

## Design principles

### Decisions drive outcomes

The target balance is approximately:

- **70%** traceable to player decisions
- **20%** calculated uncertainty
- **10%** genuine surprises

Chance should modify a good decision, not routinely erase it.

### Information is imperfect

Forecasts depend on the quality of the people and departments producing them. A better executive narrows uncertainty; they do not reveal the future.

### Success creates new problems

Growth brings larger payrolls, debt, expectations, legal exposure, employee politics, and operational complexity. The game should remain strategically demanding after the program becomes nationally recognized.

### Systems create the stories

Drama should usually emerge from the simulation:

- An unreliable supplier misses a championship deadline.
- An expensive recruit fails to fit the coaching staff.
- A neglected backup becomes a star.
- A profitable media acquisition turns into a regulatory burden.
- An aggressive expansion produces victories but destroys cash flow.

Narrative events will support these systems rather than replace them.

## Inspiration and originality

College Legends is inspired by the depth, interconnected systems, and open-ended economic management found in classic business simulation games. Its setting, mechanics, code, writing, data, and visual identity are being developed as an original work.
